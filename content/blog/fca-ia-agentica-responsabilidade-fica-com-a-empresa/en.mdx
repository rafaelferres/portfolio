On November 24, the Financial Conduct Authority (FCA), the UK's financial regulator, published a page called "Our approach to Agentic AI". It does not create new AI rules. It does something more useful for people who run these systems: it spells out how existing rules apply once an AI system stops just answering and starts acting. The core message is short: the agent can carry out the task, but accountability stays with the firm and the executives who put it into production.

## What happened

The FCA page lays out the regulator's position in a few points.

- **Accountability can't be outsourced.** Whether a firm builds an agent, deploys one, or simply buys one from a vendor, it remains responsible for meeting its regulatory obligations. Using an outside provider does not shift the burden.
- **Understanding the system is an obligation.** The FCA expects firms to be able to show they understand the systems they use, how autonomous those systems are, the risks they create, and the controls needed in real time and under stress.
- **The Consumer Duty applies to agents.** The duty to avoid foreseeable harm, give customers the information they need and monitor outcomes applies to agentic products, including governance, outcome monitoring and redress.
- **Executives answer for their own area.** Under the Senior Managers and Certification Regime (SM&CR), a senior manager is responsible for the AI models used in their area, whether built in-house or by a third party. They must understand the risks, provide oversight, challenge deployment and make sure escalation is adequate.
- **Authorisation may be needed from day one.** According to the page, some agentic products may require regulation at launch, or may evolve to the point where it applies. That happens when AI moves from providing information to initiating transactions, moving money, arranging credit or making investment decisions.
- **Customers' agents count too.** The FCA asks firms to think about how they respond when a customer uses an AI agent to deal with them: how they know they are dealing with an agent, what consents are in place, what that agent is allowed to do, and how the customer gets support if something goes wrong. On payments, firms should weigh whether it is appropriate to accept instructions from an agent or from a digital wallet it controls, and what risks they take on by accepting.
- **"Tech company" is not a shield.** Technology providers and platforms need to check whether what they do is already a regulated activity, such as payments, credit or financial advice. Calling yourself a technology provider does not settle the question.

The regulator also points to its AI Lab for testing products and says it intends to run a policy sprint on agents with firms and vendors, with no dates yet. The legal blog Regulation Tomorrow and the trade publication Insurance Business confirm the publication and its focus on accountability. One caveat: some coverage attributes stronger statements to the FCA than the page makes, such as a pledge never to write AI-specific rules. The official text only says the approach is principles-based and outcomes-focused.

## Why it matters

The FCA regulates the UK market, but the reasoning applies to any company that lets agents act on money, contracts or customer data. Three points stand out.

First, **accountability has a name and a job title**. A generic AI committee is not enough. For every agent in production, someone must be able to explain what it does, how far it can go and how it gets switched off.

Second, **the risk changes the moment the agent starts acting**. An assistant that explains a product and an agent that initiates a transfer may run on the same model, but they are not the same product. The line that matters is action, not technology.

Third, **the agent on the other side is your problem too**. Until now, most governance discussion looked at the agents a company builds. The FCA is a reminder that customers will show up with their own agents, and firms need to decide how to identify them, what to accept from them and how to support the customer when something goes wrong.

## What to do in practice

If I were reviewing an agent program today, with or without UK operations, I would do this:

1. **Inventory with an owner.** List every agent in production or pilot, the model vendor, the tools it calls and the accountable executive. An agent without an owner is the first finding of any audit.
2. **Classify by ability to act.** Separate agents that only inform from those that write to systems, move money or commit on a customer's behalf. The second group needs legal review before launch, not after.
3. **Contracts don't replace controls.** For third-party agents, require logs, configurable limits and a way to shut them off. Accountability stays with you, so visibility has to stay with you too.
4. **Test the stress scenario.** Define what happens when the model fails at scale, the vendor goes down or the policy changes: spending limits, human approval in risk bands and a tested kill switch.
5. **Prepare the door for customer agents.** Decide how your API or channel identifies agent traffic, what consent it needs to see, which operations it accepts without human confirmation and how customers complain.
6. **Measure customer outcomes, not just accuracy.** Track complaints, reversals and harm cases linked to agent decisions. That is what the regulator will ask about.

## In short

The FCA did not invent a new regime for agents. It said the current one already covers them, and that accountability sits with the firm and identifiable executives, even when the agent comes from outside. For anyone leading AI in production, the question is simple: for every agent acting on behalf of the company or the customer, who answers for it, with what limits and with what evidence?
